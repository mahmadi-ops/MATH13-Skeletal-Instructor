    Subsection 4.7.6 Why the Second Derivative Test Works

    Where do the conditions of Theorem 4.7.12 come from? The key idea is to take the directional derivative twice. At a critical point every direction looks flat to the first derivative, so we ask the second derivative how the surface curves as we walk away from the point in each direction. The video animates the argument; the equations that appear in it are the ones you will derive below.
    🔗

    Slicing the surface in a direction \(\mathbf{u}\).

    Suppose \(f(x,y)\) has continuous first and second partial derivatives near a critical point \((a,b)\text{,}\) so that \(f_x(a,b) = f_y(a,b) = 0\text{.}\) Fix a unit vector \(\mathbf{u} = (u_1, u_2)\) and walk away from the critical point along the line through \((a,b)\) in the direction of \(\mathbf{u}\text{.}\) Write down the height of the surface above that line as a single-variable function \(g(t)\text{.}\) (Parametrize the line as in (4.5.2), then feed it into \(f\text{.}\) This \(g\) is the slice cut by the vertical plane through \((a,b)\) containing \(\mathbf u\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗

    The first directional derivative.

    Differentiate \(g\) once, and evaluate at the critical point. (Use (4.4.1); the two inner derivatives are constants, read straight off the parametrization. Recognize the result as something already named in Subsection 4.5.2. Then set \(t = 0\) and use the hypothesis — the answer is the same for every \(\mathbf u\text{,}\) which is exactly why one derivative cannot tell the three cases apart. Say that in a sentence.)
    🔗
    🔗

    The second directional derivative.

    Differentiate once more. (Apply the chain rule to each of the two terms of \(g'(t)\text{,}\) remembering that \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) are themselves functions of \(x\) and \(y\text{.}\) Four second partials appear, but Theorem 4.3.11 lets two of them be combined; the result is a quadratic in \(u_1\) and \(u_2\text{.}\))
    🔗

    The Second Directional Derivative.

    \begin{equation} D^2_{\mathbf{u}}f = f_{xx}\, u_1^2 + 2 f_{xy}\, u_1 u_2 + f_{yy}\, u_2^2 .\tag{4.7.9} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    The number \(g''(0) = D^2_{\mathbf{u}}f(a,b)\) is the curvature of the slice at the critical point. Say what its sign means, and then what the single-variable second derivative test, applied to \(g\text{,}\) gives in each of three cases. (A positive value means the slice is concave one way, a negative value the other. Then ask what must be true across all directions for a local minimum, for a local maximum, and for a saddle.)
    🔗
    Writing \(\mathbf{u} = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) turns (4.7.9) into a function of the direction angle alone. Write it down, and evaluate it for the three surfaces of Example 4.7.20. (Two of the three come out constant in \(\theta\text{,}\) which is the whole story for those surfaces; the third does not, and a double-angle identity puts it in a form where you can count how often it changes sign in a full turn.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Completing the square: the discriminant appears.

    Checking the sign of (4.7.9) one direction at a time is impossible; there are infinitely many directions. Instead, assume \(f_{xx} \neq 0\text{,}\) multiply (4.7.9) by \(f_{xx}\text{,}\) and complete the square in \(u_1\text{.}\) (Multiplying first is what makes the square come out with integer coefficients. Group the \(u_1^2\) and \(u_1u_2\) terms into a perfect square, and see what has to be added back. Check your work by expanding the square again.)
    🔗

    The Completed Square.

    \begin{equation} f_{xx}\cdot D^2_{\mathbf{u}}f = \left(f_{xx} u_1 + f_{xy} u_2\right)^2 + \left(f_{xx} f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2\right) u_2^2 .\tag{4.7.10} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Now say what has been gained. (Every term on the right of (4.7.10) is a square except one coefficient — name it, and match it against (4.7.3). How many numbers now control the sign of \(D^2_{\mathbf u}f\) in all directions at once, and which are they?)
    🔗
    🔗

    Reading off the four cases.

    Work through the three cases from (4.7.10).
    🔗
    (For \(H \gt 0\text{:}\) both terms on the right are non-negative, and they cannot vanish together — check the two possibilities \(u_2 = 0\) and \(u_2 \neq 0\) separately. Conclude that \(D^2_{\mathbf u}f\) has a fixed sign, and say which. Note also that \(H \gt 0\) rules out \(f_{xx} = 0\text{;}\) show why.)
    🔗
    (For \(H \lt 0\text{:}\) the two terms now compete, so find two directions giving opposite signs. One is \(\mathbf u = (1,0)\text{;}\) for the other, choose \(\mathbf u\) so that the perfect square vanishes — the bracket in (4.7.10) tells you which direction that is.)
    🔗
    (For \(H = 0\text{:}\) what does (4.7.10) collapse to, and in which direction does it vanish? Say what the second derivative can and cannot tell you there.)
    🔗
    These are precisely conditions (i)–(iv) of Theorem 4.7.12. One technical remark: the argument above examines only straight-line slices through \((a,b)\text{,}\) which by itself is not quite enough (a function can increase along every line yet fail to have a local minimum). The full proof replaces \(f\) near \((a,b)\) by its second-order Taylor expansion; continuity of the second partials guarantees that when \(H \neq 0\) the quadratic form \(D^2_{\mathbf{u}}f\) keeps its sign uniformly on a small disk around \((a,b)\text{,}\) which upgrades the line-by-line conclusion to a genuine local one.
