    Subsection 4.8.4 Functions of \(n\) Variables

    Subsubsection 4.8.4.1 The Method in \(n\) Variables

    Nothing in the reasoning behind Theorem 4.8.4 depended on the inputs being two or three numbers. The constraint \(g = 0\) still describes a set of points on which we are allowed to move; \(\nabla g\) is still perpendicular to that set; and at a constrained extremum \(f\) still cannot be changing along any direction we are allowed to move in, so \(\nabla f\) can have no component tangent to the constraint set. As before, the only way for a vector to be perpendicular to every allowed direction is for it to point along \(\nabla g\text{.}\) So the method reads the same way for any number of variables.
    🔗

    Theorem 4.8.14. The Method of Lagrange Multipliers in \(n\) Variables.

    Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable functions of the \(n\) variables \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\text{.}\) To find the local maximum and minimum values of \(f\) subject to the constraint \(g(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = 0\text{,}\) find the values of \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\) and \(\lambda\) that simultaneously satisfy the equations
    \begin{equation} \nabla f = \lambda\, \nabla g \qquad \text{and} \qquad g(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n) = 0 .\tag{4.8.9} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Here the gradient has \(n\) components,
    \begin{equation*} \nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2},\ \ldots,\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right), \end{equation*}
    and likewise for \(\nabla g\text{.}\) Reading (4.8.9) one component at a time turns it into
    \begin{align*} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j} \amp= \lambda\, \frac{\partial g}{\partial x_j}, \qquad j = 1, 2, \ldots, n, \end{align*}
    which together with \(g = 0\) is a system of \(n+1\) equations in the \(n+1\) unknowns \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n, \lambda\text{.}\) For \(n = 2\) and \(n = 3\) this is exactly (4.8.6). The bookkeeping grows, but the strategy does not: eliminate \(\lambda\text{,}\) solve for the candidate points, and then compare the values of \(f\) there.
    🔗
    One habit does need adjusting. In Example 4.8.5 and Example 4.8.7 the constraint curve was closed and bounded, so the Extreme Value Theorem guaranteed in advance that the absolute maximum and minimum existed and were among the candidate points. In \(n\) variables a constraint set is often unbounded, and then no such guarantee comes for free: after solving the system we must argue separately that the point we found really is the extremum we are after. The example below does exactly that.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.8.4.2 An Inequality Between Two Means

    Our last application of the method is not an optimization problem at first sight: it is an inequality, valid for any number of positive numbers, which turns into a constrained optimization problem as soon as the right quantity is held fixed. For positive numbers \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\text{,}\) the harmonic mean and the geometric mean
     1 
    You are not expected to have met these averages before, and nothing later in the course depends on them. The arithmetic mean \(A = \frac{x_1 + \cdots + x_n}{n}\) is the ordinary average. The geometric mean \(G = \sqrt[n]{x_1 \cdots x_n}\) is the number that, repeated \(n\) times, has the same product: for \(n = 2\) it is the side of a square with the same area as an \(a\)-by-\(b\) rectangle, and it is the right average for growth factors, since an investment that grows by factors \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) in successive years grows in total as though it had grown by the constant factor \(G\) each year. The harmonic mean \(H = \frac{n}{1/x_1 + \cdots + 1/x_n}\) is the reciprocal of the average of the reciprocals, and it is the right average for rates: drive one mile at \(30\) mph and the next at \(60\) mph and your average speed over the two miles is the harmonic mean \(40\) mph, not the arithmetic mean \(45\) mph.
    are
    \begin{equation} H = \frac{n}{1/x_1 + 1/x_2 + \cdots + 1/x_n}, \qquad G = \sqrt[n]{x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n} .\tag{4.8.10} \end{equation}
    The example below proves that \(H \le G\) always, with equality exactly when all the numbers are the same. If you are curious about where these two averages come from, and about what the inequality amounts to for two numbers, where it can be both checked by hand and seen in a picture, read Remark 4.8.16 after the example; it is optional.
    🔗

    Example 4.8.15. The Harmonic Mean Never Exceeds the Geometric Mean.

    Let \(n \ge 2\) and let \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\) be positive real numbers. Use the method of Lagrange multipliers to prove that
    \begin{equation} \frac{n}{1/x_1 + 1/x_2 + \cdots + 1/x_n} \;\le\; \sqrt[n]{x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n},\tag{4.8.11} \end{equation}
    that is, \(H \le G\text{,}\) with equality if and only if \(x_1 = x_2 = \cdots = x_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    One last observation costs nothing and doubles the yield. (Apply (4.8.11) not to the numbers \(x_j\) themselves but to their reciprocals \(y_j = 1/x_j\text{.}\) Work out what the harmonic mean and the geometric mean of the \(y_j\) are in terms of the arithmetic mean \(A\) and the geometric mean \(G\) of the \(x_j\text{,}\) then invert the resulting inequality — both sides are positive, so the direction flips.)
    🔗

    The Three Means.

    Applying (4.8.11) to the reciprocals gives the celebrated inequality between the geometric and arithmetic means,
    \begin{equation} \sqrt[n]{x_1x_2\cdots x_n} \;\le\; \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n} ,\tag{4.8.13} \end{equation}
    again with equality exactly when all the \(x_j\) are equal. Together with the example this establishes the full chain
    \begin{equation} H \le G \le A\tag{4.8.14} \end{equation}
    for any number of positive numbers—the general form of what Figure 4.8.18 shows for two.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 4.8.16. For the Curious: the Two-Variable Case and a Picture.

    This remark is optional, and nothing later in the course depends on it. It is here for the reader curious about what the inequality just proved actually says. For two numbers it can be checked with nothing but algebra and, better, read off a single picture, along with the arithmetic mean for good measure.
    🔗
    For \(n = 2\) no calculus is needed at all. Write the two positive numbers as \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation} H = \frac{2}{\frac1a + \frac1b} = \frac{2ab}{a+b}, \qquad G = \sqrt{ab},\tag{4.8.15} \end{equation}
    and since \(a + b \gt 0\) and \(\sqrt{ab} \gt 0\) we may multiply and divide freely without reversing the inequality:
    \begin{align*} \frac{2ab}{a+b} \le \sqrt{ab} \amp\;\Longleftrightarrow\; 2ab \le \sqrt{ab}\,(a+b)\\ \amp\;\Longleftrightarrow\; 2\sqrt{ab} \le a + b\\ \amp\;\Longleftrightarrow\; 0 \le a - 2\sqrt{ab} + b = \left(\sqrt a - \sqrt b\,\right)^2 , \end{align*}
    where the second line divides through by \(\sqrt{ab} \gt 0\text{,}\) using \(ab = \sqrt{ab}\cdot\sqrt{ab}\text{.}\) The last line is true for every choice of \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) and it is an equality exactly when \(\sqrt a = \sqrt b\text{,}\) that is, when \(a = b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The middle line of that chain is worth a second look. It says \(\sqrt{ab} \le \frac{a+b}{2}\text{,}\) so for two numbers the statement \(H \le G\) is the very same statement as \(G \le A\text{,}\) where \(A\) is the arithmetic mean. The two-variable case therefore carries the whole chain (4.8.14) on its own, without any appeal to (4.8.13).
    🔗
    All three means can be read off a single picture, and the geometric mean is the interesting one. It comes from a fact about right triangles: the altitude drawn from the right angle to the hypotenuse is the geometric mean of the two pieces into which it cuts the hypotenuse. In Figure 4.8.17 the right angle is at \(C\text{,}\) the altitude \(CD\) has length \(h\text{,}\) and it splits the hypotenuse into pieces of lengths \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\) The two small triangles \(ADC\) and \(CDB\) are both similar to the whole triangle \(ACB\text{,}\) hence to each other, so matching the short leg of one against the long leg of the other gives
    \begin{equation} \frac{a}{h} = \frac{h}{b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h^2 = ab \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \sqrt{ab} = G .\tag{4.8.16} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Now lay the two lengths \(a\) and \(b\) end to end and draw the semicircle having the resulting segment as its diameter, as in Figure 4.8.18. The radius is \(\frac{a+b}{2} = A\text{,}\) drawn vertically at the center \(O\text{.}\) Any triangle inscribed in a semicircle has a right angle at the point on the arc, so the vertical segment \(DC\) erected where \(a\) meets \(b\) is precisely the altitude of (4.8.16): its length is \(G = \sqrt{ab}\text{.}\) Finally drop a perpendicular from \(D\) to the radius \(OC\text{,}\) meeting it at \(M\text{.}\) Applying (4.8.16) a second time, now to the right triangle \(ODC\) with its right angle at \(D\text{,}\) gives \(DC^2 = CM \cdot CO\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} CM = \frac{DC^2}{CO} = \frac{G^2}{A} = \frac{ab}{\frac{a+b}{2}} = \frac{2ab}{a+b} = H . \end{equation*}
    The chain (4.8.14) is now visible rather than computed: \(CM \le CD\) because a leg of the right triangle \(CMD\) cannot exceed its hypotenuse, and \(CD \le CO\) for the same reason in the right triangle \(ODC\text{.}\) Both become equalities exactly when \(D\) coincides with \(O\text{,}\) that is, when \(a = b\text{.}\)
